\partwithintro[part:bridge]{The Bridge: From Signals to Representations}{%
Part~\ref{part:foundation} focused on modeling brain signals. This part develops a connected argument for bridging fMRI and visual content through language. \emph{\prism{} I} investigates the choice of alignment space and finds stronger alignment with language-model semantic representations among the alternatives evaluated. Motivated by this choice, \mindllm{} learns fMRI-to-language alignment across subjects and tasks. \emph{\prism{} II} then examines how language can support visual reconstruction, structuring the representation into objects, attributes, and relations and generating scenes object by object. This object-disentangled semantic space motivates a generator that preserves and strengthens the separation between items. \dedit{} develops that generative side beyond \prism{}'s object-centric scene construction, using learned per-item prompts and grouped cross-attention to provide more expressive, disentangled visual control. The argument thus proceeds from choosing an alignment space, to learning the alignment, to reconstructing through language, and to strengthening the object-disentangled generator. The two \prism{} contributions are presented together in one chapter; coupling the learned neural decoder to \dedit{} remains future work.}

\chapter{Seeing Through the Brain: New Insights from Decoding Visual Stimuli with fMRI}
\label{chapter:prism}

\prism{} makes two contributions to the semantic bridge developed in this part. \emph{\prism{} I} asks which representation space provides a useful alignment target for fMRI. Its comparison of vision, language, and vision-language representations found stronger alignment with language-model representations among the evaluated alternatives. This observation motivates the fMRI-to-language alignment studied by \mindllm{} in Chapter~\ref{chapter:mindllm}. \emph{\prism{} II} addresses the subsequent question in the argument: how can language support visual reconstruction? It describes a scene through objects, attributes, and relations and adapts a diffusion model to compose the objects into an image. Both contributions are presented in this chapter, while the argument across the part follows \prism{} I, \mindllm{}, \prism{} II, and \dedit{}. The object-disentangled structure used for reconstruction provides the motivation for strengthening the generator's item-level control in Chapter~\ref{chapter:dedit}.

\chapter{\mindllm{}: A Subject-Agnostic and Versatile Model for fMRI-to-Text Decoding}
\label{chapter:mindllm}

\prism{} I motivates language as an alignment space for fMRI. This chapter develops the mapping into that space: \mindllm{} aligns brain recordings with language through a subject-agnostic fMRI encoder and brain instruction tuning. The encoder accommodates differences in voxel number and spatial distribution across subjects, while instruction tuning connects recordings with diverse question annotations through shared stimulus images. This supports a common language interface for tasks involving perception, memory, language, and reasoning. In the argument of this part, \mindllm{} supplies the alignment-learning step between the choice of space in \prism{} I and language-mediated visual reconstruction in \prism{} II. Reconstruction then makes the structure of the language space consequential: objects, attributes, and relations need to remain distinct as they are expressed visually. This motivates the more expressive object-disentangled generator studied next.

\chapter{An Item is Worth a Prompt: Versatile Image Editing with Disentangled Control}
\label{chapter:dedit}

The bridge from fMRI through language to visual reconstruction depends on retaining the structure of its semantic representation. \prism{} II separates a scene into objects, attributes, and relations and uses object-centric generation to express that structure visually. This motivates stronger disentanglement in the generator itself. \dedit{} extends the generative side of the bridge by learning a separate prompt for each image item and using grouped cross-attention so that each image region attends to its associated prompt. This gives the generator more expressive item-level control, supporting text-, reference-image-, and mask-guided editing as well as item removal. The contribution is a broader object-disentangled generation framework that develops the control available from \prism{}'s object-centric construction. The chapter evaluates this generative capability on image-editing tasks; connecting it to decoded fMRI representations remains a future integration step.
